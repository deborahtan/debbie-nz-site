How I use AI

I work in data and I build with AI most days. That doesn't make me a cheerleader for it. This is where I've landed: what I actually use it for, what I refuse to hand over, and the bar a thing has to clear before I'll call it a win.

Short version: it's a tool, not a brain transplant. I want it making me faster at work I understand, not confident about work I don't.

  1. 1 🛠️ It's a tool, not a brain transplantI use it to go faster on things I already understand — not to have an opinion on my behalf.

    The line I hold is simple: AI drafts, I decide. It's very good at the first 70% of something tedious — a first pass at a tracking plan, boilerplate SQL, turning my notes into something readable. It is not good at knowing whether the thing is worth doing, which is the part people actually pay for.

    The failure mode isn't a wrong answer. It's a plausible one. A fluent, well-formatted, confidently-wrong answer is far more dangerous in measurement work than an obvious error, because nobody questions a tidy-looking number until it's in a board deck.

  2. 2 🧠 Don't outsource the thinkingIf I let it do the reasoning, I lose the ability to tell when it's wrong.

    This is the one I'm genuinely careful about. The skill that makes me useful is noticing when something smells off — a conversion rate that's too clean, an attribution shift nobody can explain. That instinct comes from having done the work by hand enough times. Hand all of it over and the instinct quietly goes.

    So I still read the query. I still check the numbers against a source I trust. When I use it on something I don't know well, I treat the output as a hypothesis to go and verify, not an answer. The 2026 research on this is pretty blunt: fluent output creates an illusion of competence, and unstructured use trends toward outsourcing rather than learning.

  3. 3 🧪 Build things I'd actually useEverything on my roadmap exists because the manual version annoyed me first.

    I don't build to have built something. Every tool I've made started as a task I'd done by hand enough times to be sure of the shape of it — writing measurement plans, hand-building containers, checking UTM naming. If I haven't felt the pain, I don't know enough to automate it well, and I'll automate the wrong step.

    The test I apply: would I reach for this next week, unprompted? If the honest answer is no, it's a demo, not a tool. Demos are fine — I just don't pretend they're products. Plenty of AI output looks finished and simply moves the work onto whoever receives it.

  4. 4 💰 If I can't measure it, it isn't a winTime saved, cost to run, and what it changed commercially — or it's a hobby.

    I measure the tools I build the same way I'd measure a campaign. What did it cost to run, including the tokens and my time building it? How many hours did it give back, honestly, counting the checking? What decision got made faster or better because of it?

    Being in measurement, I find the industry-wide version of this a bit uncomfortable: spending is up and the financial impact mostly isn't showing. I'd rather have three automations with a number next to them than a dozen impressive-looking things nobody can defend in a budget conversation.

    The strategic case matters too, and it's not the same as the cost case. Some things earn their place by making the work consistent, or by removing a single point of failure, or by making a capability sellable. Those are real — they just need to be argued on their own terms rather than smuggled in under 'efficiency'.

  5. 5 ⚖️ Measured, and a bit scepticalKnowing when not to use it is part of using it well.

    There's work I deliberately keep manual. Anything where the thinking is the deliverable — a strategy, a recommendation someone will spend money on, a judgement call about a person — I do myself. Not out of principle, but because the reasoning is the value, and I can't stand behind reasoning I didn't do.

    I'm also not interested in being the person who's either breathlessly for it or reflexively against it. Both positions save you from having to evaluate anything. The useful position is boring: try it, measure it, keep what works, drop what doesn't, and be honest when something you built turns out not to be worth it.

    And where I use it as a sounding board rather than an oracle, it's genuinely good — it argues back, it catches things, it makes me articulate why I think what I think. That's the version I'd recommend.
